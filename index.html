DNS Ad Blocker — Technical Documentation

Every device on your network — phones, laptops, smart TVs — makes hundreds of DNS requests every day. Many of those requests are for ads, trackers, and content you never asked for. This project intercepts those requests at the network level and blocks them before they ever reach your devices, using OpenDNS for category filtering and Pi-hole for ad blocking. No browser extensions. No per-device setup. One solution that covers everything, running on a repurposed 2010 iMac as a dedicated server.

— PART 1 — OpenDNS —

01. What is DNS?

Think of the internet as a massive city. Every website has a real address — an IP address — but nobody memorizes those. Instead, we use names like youtube.com. DNS is the system that translates those names into the actual addresses your device needs to make a connection. It works exactly like a phone book, except it is for the entire internet.

Here is what actually happens when you open your browser and type youtube.com. Your device has no idea where that is, so it asks your router. The router does not know either, so it forwards the question to a DNS server — usually one provided by your ISP. That server looks up the address, returns the real IP, and now your device knows exactly where to go. The whole process takes milliseconds.

How blocking works at the DNS level

A filtering DNS server answers a blocked query according to its configured policy instead of returning the domain's normal address. Depending on the product and mode, the response may contain a null or sinkhole address, an NXDOMAIN or NODATA response, or a vendor-specific block-page address. Pi-hole's documented blocking modes include NULL, IP-NODATA-AAAA, IP, NXDOMAIN, and NODATA. In each case, the client does not receive the intended destination address.

02. What is OpenDNS?

OpenDNS is a Cisco-operated DNS service that lets you apply filtering policies to DNS requests from your network. Devices that accept DNS settings from the router can use it without individual configuration, while devices using manual DNS settings, VPN-provided DNS, or encrypted DNS such as DoH may bypass the router-provided resolver. From the OpenDNS dashboard you can configure category and domain filtering policies.

OpenDNS can provide more control than an ISP-provided resolver through its dashboard, filtering rules, and optional reporting. DNS performance is not universal: latency depends on location, routing, cache state, and the ISP, so OpenDNS may be faster or slower on a particular network. Measure both resolvers before claiming a performance improvement.

03. Creating an OpenDNS Account

  1. Go to opendns.com and select the Consumer section.
  2. From the free plan options, choose the one on the right — the customizable version.
  3. Click Sign Up and fill in your information to create the account.
  4. Once logged in, OpenDNS will display your current public IP at the top of the dashboard. That IP is how OpenDNS identifies your network and applies your custom settings.
Keep in mind that home ISPs sometimes change your public IP. If OpenDNS stops working after some time, log back in and update the IP associated with your network.

04. Configuring the Router

A common way to apply OpenDNS broadly is to advertise it through the router's DHCP settings. Clients that accept those DNS settings will use OpenDNS without individual configuration. Clients with manually configured DNS, VPN-provided DNS, or encrypted DNS can use a different resolver.

OpenDNS server addresses

Primary DNS server
208.67.222.222

Secondary DNS server (for redundancy)
208.67.220.220

What to do on the router

Every router is different, but the goal is the same: find the DHCP settings and replace the current DNS server addresses with the OpenDNS ones above. On most routers this is found under a section called Advanced, Network, or LAN Setup. OpenDNS provides step-by-step guides for almost every router model at opendns.com/setupguide.

After saving the router settings, connected devices will not switch over immediately. They may keep using the previous DNS settings until their DHCP lease renews. The lease duration depends on the DHCP server. To request updated settings immediately on a Windows machine, run these two commands in Command Prompt: 
Release the current network lease
ipconfig /release

Request a new lease with the updated DNS
ipconfig /renew

05. Customizing the Filtering

Adding your network to the dashboard

  1. In the OpenDNS dashboard, click Add a Network.
  2. It will auto-detect your public IP. Confirm it and click Add this network.
  3. Give it a name you will recognize, then click the network to open its settings.

Web content filtering

OpenDNS lets you block entire categories of websites in one click. You can choose a preset protection level or go custom and pick exactly which categories to block. Useful categories include adult content, malware sites, phishing, and social media.

OpenDNS offers preset filtering levels and custom category selection. Preset contents can change over time, so review the categories shown in the current OpenDNS dashboard instead of relying on a static list in this write-up.

Stats and Logs

OpenDNS dashboard statistics are not displayed until Stats and Logs is enabled for the network. Enabling it makes DNS activity and filtering reports available in the dashboard. That setting should not be described as proof that Cisco keeps no operational records; review the OpenDNS Home terms and provide notice to household users before enabling activity reporting.

Give Stats and Logs a few hours to collect data before checking the dashboard. After a day or two, the patterns in your network traffic become very clear.

06. Verification

Once the router is configured and your device has renewed its network lease, you can confirm that OpenDNS is active by visiting the official setup guide page. On the right side of opendns.com/setupguide there is a section that reads "Test Your Settings" with a link that checks whether your network is correctly routing DNS through OpenDNS. Click it and you will get an immediate confirmation.

Test a blocked site from the browser

  1. Wait about 3 minutes after saving your filtering settings for them to propagate.
  2. Open a private or incognito window to avoid cached results.
  3. Try to visit a domain you have set to block. Instead of the site loading, you will see the OpenDNS block page.

Test from the terminal

Query a domain you explicitly blocked in OpenDNS
nslookup <blocked-domain>

Verify the policy result in the browser or resolver output. Do not rely on one hard-coded block-page IP address because resolver behavior and blocking responses can change.

If the test page confirms your connection and blocked sites show the OpenDNS block page instead of loading, Part 1 is fully working.

07. Full Architecture Overview

Part 2 adds Pi-hole — a network-wide DNS ad blocker installed directly on a dedicated machine. The server is a 2010 iMac running Zorin OS, managed remotely over SSH from a Windows PC. Pi-hole receives DNS queries from clients configured to use it and blocks matching ad and tracker domains before those destinations are resolved normally. Permitted queries are forwarded to OpenDNS, which applies the upstream filtering policy from Part 1.

08. Initial Setup on Zorin OS

Zorin OS is already installed on the 2010 iMac with its desktop environment. Zorin is Ubuntu-based, but it is not one of the distributions Pi-hole explicitly lists as tested; this write-up documents the configuration that worked in this lab. The first step is making sure the system is fully up to date and that SSH is enabled for remote administration.

Update and upgrade all packages

Update the package list and upgrade installed packages
sudo apt update && sudo apt upgrade -y

Enable and start the SSH service

Install the OpenSSH server if not already present
sudo apt install openssh-server -y

Enable SSH so it starts automatically on every boot
sudo systemctl enable ssh

Start the SSH service right now without rebooting
sudo systemctl start ssh

Confirm the service is running
sudo systemctl status ssh
The status output should show active (running). Once SSH is up, the iMac can be managed entirely from the Windows PC.

Find the iMac's local IP address

Check the network interface and note the IPv4 address
ip a

Write down that IP address. You will need it to connect via SSH and later to set up the static reservation on the router.

09. Connecting from Windows via SSH

With SSH running on the iMac, open a terminal on the Windows PC and connect remotely. From this point on, all commands are run through this SSH session. 

Connect to the iMac from the Windows PC
ssh yourusername@<iMac-IP>

Stay in the regular account and use sudo only for commands that require administrative privileges. A persistent root shell is unnecessary for this setup.

Install curl

This walkthrough uses curl to download the Pi-hole installer for review before execution.

Install curl
sudo apt install curl -y
PuTTY is a popular and free SSH client for Windows. The built-in Windows Terminal also works — just open it and run the ssh command directly.

10. Setting a Static IP Reservation on the Router

Before using the iMac as the network DNS server, give it a stable local address. A static address or DHCP reservation prevents the resolver address from changing and breaking DNS for clients that depend on it.

The cleanest way to handle this is through a DHCP reservation on the router. Log into the router admin panel, find the connected devices list, locate the iMac, and assign it a permanent IP tied to its MAC address. Every router does this slightly differently, but the setting is usually found under DHCP, LAN, or Connected Devices.

Set the reservation before finishing the installation. The address the installer detects during setup is the one used for the web panel URL, the router DNS settings, and every device pointing to Pi-hole.

11. Installing Pi-hole

Pi-hole is installed with its official installer from docs.pi-hole.net/main/basic-install. Download it first so its contents can be reviewed before it is executed with administrator privileges. 

Download, review, and run the official Pi-hole installer
curl --proto '=https' --tlsv1.2 -fsSLo basic-install.sh https://install.pi-hole.net
less basic-install.sh
sudo bash basic-install.sh
rm -f basic-install.sh
Never pipe a remote script directly into a shell. Review the downloaded file before running it, and obtain it only from Pi-hole's official HTTPS endpoint.

The installer walks through a short setup process. The prompts can vary by Pi-hole version; these were the selections used for the reviewed lab installation:

Set a custom admin password

When the installer finishes it shows a generated password. Set your own right away:

The web panel is now accessible at http://<iMac-IP>/admin. Log in with the password you just set to confirm everything installed correctly.

12. Adding Community Blocklists

The default Pi-hole blocklist is a solid starting point, but adding community-maintained lists significantly expands coverage. Two additional lists were added to this setup, both known for being comprehensive and well-maintained.

How to add a blocklist

  1. Open the Pi-hole web panel and go to Adlists under the Group Management section.
  2. Paste the URL of the blocklist into the address field and click Add.
  3. After adding both lists, go to Tools and click Update Gravity to pull in the new entries.

13. Pointing the Router to Pi-hole

Pi-hole is running and working, but at this point only clients configured to use it will benefit. To make it the default resolver for the network, configure the router's DHCP settings to advertise the Pi-hole address to clients.

Log into the router admin panel and find the DNS settings under DHCP or WAN configuration. Set the primary DNS to the iMac's static IP address. Leave the secondary slot blank unless you run a second Pi-hole. Configure OpenDNS as an upstream resolver inside Pi-hole instead of advertising it directly to clients; otherwise, some devices may bypass Pi-hole filtering.

DNS SlotAddressPurpose
Primary DNSiMac static IP (Pi-hole)Router-advertised DNS points clients to Pi-hole
Secondary DNSBlank, or a second Pi-holeAvoids bypassing network-wide filtering
Once the router is saved, clients that accept the router's DHCP-provided DNS settings will start using Pi-hole after they renew their lease. Devices using manual DNS, VPN DNS, or encrypted DNS may continue to use a different resolver.

14. Verification

With Pi-hole running and the router updated, ad and tracker requests should be reduced on clients that are using Pi-hole as their resolver.

Visual test in the browser

Open any news site or content-heavy page that normally shows a lot of ads and do a hard refresh. A noticeable portion of the ad slots will be blank or missing. The page may also load faster when blocked requests remove unnecessary network activity.

Check the Pi-hole dashboard

Open http://<iMac-IP>/admin and log in. The dashboard shows the total number of DNS queries processed and the percentage that was blocked. The Query Log can show individual requests when the configured privacy and retention settings permit it; blocked ones are clearly marked. Once the router change takes effect you may see queries arriving from multiple devices on the network, not just the one you tested on.

The blocked percentage varies widely with the clients, applications, browsing patterns, and blocklists in use. Use the dashboard to establish a baseline for this network instead of treating any fixed percentage as typical.

15. Final Thoughts

I want to be honest about why this iMac ended up as the server for this project. It was sitting there, barely used, and it felt wrong to just let it collect dust. I know a 2010 iMac is not the most power-efficient machine for a task like this — a Raspberry Pi would draw a fraction of the electricity — but for me the decision was never really about efficiency. It was about putting something I already owned to work for something that actually matters to my family.

That something is privacy. I think about privacy and security the same way I think about a fence around a home. It is not there because you have something to hide. It is there because you care about what is inside. You want to protect your space, your people, and the things that are yours. There is nothing wrong with that — in fact, I think it is one of the most responsible things you can do. Every ad network and tracker that gets blocked is one less thing quietly watching what your family does online.
